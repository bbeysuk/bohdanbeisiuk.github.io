Cold Email Deliverability Audit in 2026: What to Check Before Scaling

November 21, 20269 min readBy Bohdan Beisiuk

Your campaign platform says the emails were sent. Replies are scarce. Someone suggests replacing the subject line, another person suggests a new domain, and the team starts debating how many messages to send next week.

Before changing volume, determine what actually happened to the messages. A campaign can have a technical delivery problem, a targeting problem or an offer problem. Those require different fixes, and several can exist at the same time.

A cold email deliverability audit checks authentication, sending infrastructure, recipient data, provider responses and complaint handling. Its purpose is to find why messages are rejected, deferred or filtered. Successful transmission does not prove inbox placement, and inbox placement does not prove that the offer is relevant.

This November 2026 guide is for founders and business development leaders who need to diagnose an outbound campaign with their email administrator. It combines current provider documentation with my recommended operating process. It does not promise inbox placement or prescribe a universal daily sending allowance.

Separate sending, acceptance and commercial response

Agree on the meaning of your dashboard labels before interpreting results. “Sent” may mean that the campaign tool handed a message to a sending service. “Delivered” commonly reflects server acceptance rather than a confirmed appearance in the primary inbox. Read your tool's definitions instead of assuming that every platform measures the same event.

Ask for the raw delivery events where available. Permanent rejection, temporary deferral, out-of-office response, human reply and unsubscribe should be separate categories. If they are combined, a reassuring aggregate number can hide the failure you need to fix.

The commercial layer comes later. Once there is reasonable evidence that messages are reaching recipients, assess account fit, buyer responsibility and the value of the proposed conversation. My article on replies without qualified meetings covers that different diagnostic problem.

What the current Gmail and Outlook requirements actually cover

Provider rules have different scopes. Avoid turning a requirement for consumer mailboxes or high-volume senders into a claim about every business email account.

Google: personal Gmail recipients

Google's sender guidelines require SPF or DKIM for all senders to personal Gmail accounts. Bulk senders face additional requirements, including SPF and DKIM, DMARC and alignment. Marketing and subscribed messages in that category need one-click unsubscribe and a visible unsubscribe link. Google also specifies TLS, DNS and spam-rate requirements.

The official FAQ says enforcement increased from November 2025. It describes bulk status around 5,000 messages to personal Gmail accounts in 24 hours, aggregated across the same primary domain, and states that this classification is permanent once met. It distinguishes personal Gmail recipients from Google Workspace recipients.

Microsoft: Outlook.com recipients

Microsoft's current postmaster policy requires SPF, DKIM and DMARC for domains sending more than 5,000 daily messages to Outlook.com accounts, effective May 5, 2025. It describes junk placement and possible rejection for unresolved non-compliance. Read this as an Outlook.com policy, not a universal threshold for all Microsoft 365 business tenants.

These are existing rules checked on November 21, 2026, rather than newly announced November 2026 changes. Meeting authentication requirements does not grant permission to send unwanted messages or guarantee inbox placement.

Map every system that sends from your domain

Start with an inventory: individual mailboxes, the CRM, campaign software, newsletters, transactional messages and any external service using your brand's domain. Record the owner and purpose of each stream. Include tools that were tested and later abandoned if they still have access or configuration.

For each active stream, capture the visible From address, the sending provider and a sample message's authentication results. An email administrator should verify configuration against the relevant provider documentation. The fact that your regular mailbox works does not establish that an external campaign tool is configured correctly.

Do not paste a generic DNS record from a blog into production. The correct configuration depends on the services authorised to send for your organisation. Document the proposed change and its owner so that fixing one campaign does not unexpectedly interrupt invoices, password resets or customer communication.

Check real message authentication, not just setup screens

Ask your administrator to inspect the headers of messages from the actual sending path. A screenshot saying “domain connected” is an incomplete test. You need to know whether the message received by the destination passed the expected checks and whether the relevant domains aligned.

Keep the test controlled: use mailboxes you own or recipients who have agreed to help. Record the sending service, time and configuration version. Repeat after a material change to the sending path. One successful example demonstrates that particular path; it does not prove that every tool and campaign uses it.

A useful audit finding describes the observed evidence and the owner of the fix. “Authentication problem” is too vague. “Messages sent by this campaign service fail the expected check, while normal mailbox messages pass” gives the administrator a defined starting point.

Read failures by recipient provider and campaign cohort

Group delivery events by recipient provider, sending identity, campaign and date. A problem concentrated in one destination is different from failures across every destination. A new account list failing while an established stream remains stable also suggests a different investigation.

Store the full response code and accompanying text where your service exposes them. A permanent rejection is not the same as a temporary deferral. Ask the administrator to interpret the code before deciding whether a configuration fix, data correction or pause is required.

Microsoft's postmaster policy explicitly says not to retransmit the same message to a recipient after a permanent 500–599 response. More broadly, avoid repeatedly pushing a failing batch through the system. Identify the cause and document the corrective action before resuming affected activity.

Do not respond to filtering by cycling through identities to evade restrictions. A new address does not explain why the original campaign failed. It can also leave the team with more infrastructure to maintain and less clarity about the source of the problem.

Review contact quality and suppression together

Look beyond whether an address is syntactically valid. Is the person still at the company? Is the company in your target segment? Does the role connect to the offer? When was the information checked, and where did it come from?

Keep hard failures, opt-outs and other exclusions in a central suppression process. A contact removed from one sequence should not reappear because someone imports the same list into another tool. Assign an owner for synchronising these records across the CRM and sending platforms.

Separate public availability from outreach suitability. A published business address helps identify a route to the organisation; it does not by itself settle the provider-policy or legal requirements for your campaign. Check those requirements for the market and channel you are using.

Audit the message and the path after the click

Read your email as an unfamiliar recipient would. Is the sender identifiable? Does the subject accurately describe the message? Is the reason for contacting this person visible without a long introduction? Can they decline easily?

Check links, redirects and the destination page. The website should explain the same service as the email. A vague email promising “growth” followed by an unrelated landing page creates a trust problem even if the message arrives successfully.

I recommend testing a simple message with one commercial question when diagnosing a campaign. That makes the response easier to interpret. It is not a technical rule that short messages, plain formatting or a particular word count automatically bypass filters.

Also inspect the reply workflow. If a person responds to an address nobody monitors, the campaign can appear unsuccessful for an operational reason. Confirm who receives replies and how referrals, objections and genuine interest move into the CRM.

Use a decision log before increasing activity

A practical audit should produce a short decision log rather than a collection of screenshots. For every issue, record the observation, likely explanation, evidence still needed, corrective action, owner and condition for restarting the affected activity.

Authentication failsPause the affected sending path; have the administrator verify configuration and retest actual messages.
One list performs poorlyReview its source, freshness, role relevance and exclusion handling before another import.
Acceptance, no interestInvestigate placement where possible, then account selection and offer relevance; do not assume a copy change is enough.
Interest, no next stepCheck response ownership, qualification and scheduling rather than treating this as a delivery issue.

For a small pilot, review each reply and failure individually. Compare batches only when their definitions are consistent. Five responses from one segment and none from another may be worth investigating, but a small sample does not establish a dependable performance benchmark.

My outreach-team experience informs this emphasis on ownership: a researcher, campaign operator and sales lead should know which part of the process each is responsible for. Deliverability becomes harder to diagnose when everyone sees the dashboard but nobody owns the exceptions.

Frequently asked questions

Does “sent” mean my cold email reached the inbox?

No. A sending event does not establish recipient-server acceptance or inbox placement. Check your platform's event definitions and any available delivery responses.

Will SPF, DKIM and DMARC guarantee inbox placement?

No. Authentication is a technical foundation. Recipient response, sending behaviour, message relevance and other filtering factors still matter.

How many cold emails can I safely send each day?

There is no universal safe allowance. Provider limits, sending history, infrastructure, recipient expectations and applicable rules differ. Diagnose the current stream before deciding whether to increase it.

Should I change the domain if replies stop?

Not as an automatic first step. Examine delivery events, authentication, contact quality and the offer. A domain change without a diagnosis can repeat the same problem.
